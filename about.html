How is this 16-type profile read?

What is this profile?

This page offers a 16-type personality profile. In everyday language, that is a four-letter reading of how you tend to direct your energy, take in information, come to a decision, and organise your days.

In the deeper sense, the letters are a Jungian-style way of talking about those four pairs: Extraversion or Introversion (E or I), Sensing or Intuition (S or N), Thinking or Feeling (T or F), and Judging or Perceiving (J or P). The working definition used here is simple. Each letter is read from a public-domain personality scale, not from a proprietary questionnaire.

In other words, the four-letter type on the results page is a convenient summary of four scale scores, not a claim that you are only one kind of person.

What this section has shown is the scope of the profile: a Jungian-style reading, offered as a first look, not as a complete account of personality.

Where do the questions come from?

The statements come from the International Personality Item Pool, a public-domain collection of personality items. The 40 statements used here are the Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, and Intellect/Imagination items from the 50-item Big-Five factor markers reported by Goldberg (1992). The fifth marker, Emotional Stability, is left out of this profile, because it does not map onto one of the four letters.

The items may be used for any purpose, commercial or non-commercial. That permission is already granted on the IPIP site. The wording of each statement is used as published.

In other words, this is not a home-made quiz. It is a documented public-domain questionnaire, read in a Jungian-style way.

First, the statements themselves belong to the International Personality Item Pool. Second, the mapping of the four scales onto the four letter-pairs is a reading, not a separate official instrument. Third, the fuller assessment in this practice remains the Big Five on big5.drelijah.org, which uses the IPIP-NEO-120 and includes all five traits and their facets.

This section has established the source and the limit of the claim: public-domain items, a Jungian-style reading, and a clear path to the fuller test.

How is a four-letter type produced?

Each of the four scales has ten statements. You rate each statement on a five-point scale, from Very Inaccurate to Very Accurate. Some statements are worded in the opposite direction and are reversed when scored. Each scale is then added up. The midpoint of a scale is 30, on a range from 10 to 50.

A score above 30 is read as Extraversion, Intuition, Feeling, or Judging, according to the scale. A score below 30 is read as Introversion, Sensing, Thinking, or Perceiving. A score of exactly 30 is treated as evenly balanced; the second letter of that pair is shown, and the result marks the pair as balanced.

In other words, the type is simply which side of the midpoint you fell on, for each of the four scales, with an honest note when you landed on the midpoint itself.

The results page also shows how far each score sits from the midpoint, so a narrow preference is not presented as a strong one.

Taken together, the scoring rule is now on the table: four sums, a midpoint of 30, and a visible measure of how clear each preference is.

What should you do with the result?

Use the four-letter type as a first sketch, then take the Big Five assessment. The Big Five describes the same ground in more detail, including the fifth trait, and it is the instrument this practice uses when a fuller reading is needed.

This profile is for self-understanding and conversation. It is not psychological advice, not a clinical diagnosis, and not a basis for hiring or promotion.

In other words, the letters are a doorway. The Big Five is the room.
